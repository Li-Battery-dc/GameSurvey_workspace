BotzoneBench: Scalable LLM Evaluation via Graded AI Anchors

Lingfeng Li1    Yunlong Lu1    Yuefei Zhang2    Jingyu Yao3    Yixin Zhu4    KeYuan Cheng1    Yongyi Wang1    Qirui Zheng1    Xionghui Yang1    Wenxin Li1*

Abstract

Large Language Models are increasingly deployed in interactive environments requiring strategic decision-making, yet systematic evaluation of these capabilities remains challenging. Existing benchmarks for LLMs primarily assess static reasoning through isolated tasks and fail to capture dynamic strategic abilities. Recent game-based evaluations employ LLM-vs-LLM tournaments that produce relative rankings dependent on transient model pools, incurring quadratic computational costs and lacking stable performance anchors for longitudinal tracking. The central challenge is establishing a scalable evaluation framework that measures LLM strategic reasoning against consistent, interpretable standards rather than volatile peer models. Here we show that anchoring LLM evaluation to fixed hierarchies of skill-calibrated game Artificial Intelligence (AI) enables linear-time absolute skill measurement with stable cross-temporal interpretability. Built on the Botzone platform’s established competitive infrastructure, our BotzoneBench evaluates LLMs across eight diverse games spanning deterministic perfect-information board games to stochastic imperfect-information card games. Through systematic assessment of 177,047 state-action pairs from five flagship models, we reveal significant performance disparities and identify distinct strategic behaviors, with top-performing models achieving proficiency comparable to mid-to-high-tier specialized game AI in multiple domains. This anchored evaluation paradigm generalizes beyond games to any domain with well-defined skill hierarchies, establishing a scalable and reusable framework for assessing interactive AI capabilities.

1 Introduction

As LLMs continue to advance, researchers have developed increasingly diverse benchmarks to evaluate their emergent capabilities Moradi et al. (2025). Static benchmarks such as MATH Hendrycks et al. (2021) for mathematical reasoning, HumanEval Chen et al. (2021) for code generation, and MMLU Hendrycks et al. (2020) for general knowledge have proven valuable for assessing isolated, single-turn reasoning. However, they inherently fail to capture LLMs performance in dynamic, interactive decision-making settings. Games, with their well-defined rules, clear objectives, and demand for strategic planning, offer a natural testbed for probing multi-step reasoning, long-horizon planning, and adaptive behavior under uncertainty Bellemare et al. (2013); Silver et al. (2016); Vinyals et al. (2019).

Recently, game-based benchmarks have emerged to assess the interactive abilities of LLMs Costarelli et al. (2024); Huang et al. (2025); Guo et al. (2025); Gui et al. (2025). These approaches typically employ LLM-vs-LLM tournaments to evaluate strategic reasoning, spatial understanding, and opponent modeling. While they provide initial insights, they suffer from three fundamental limitations: (i) relative rankings dependent on transient model pools lack absolute interpretability; (ii) quadratic computational cost (O​(N2)O(N^{2}) pairwise matches) hinders scalability; and (iii) unstable baselines make longitudinal tracking of model progress across generations infeasible.

To address these challenges, we introduce BotzoneBench, a scalable evaluation framework built on the mature Botzone platform111Botzone (https://www.botzone.org.cn) hosts over 23,000 registered users, 213,000 AI bots, and 7 million matches played.. As illustrated in Figure 1, BotzoneBench employs an anchored evaluation methodology: LLMs are assessed against a fixed hierarchy of skill-calibrated, non-LLM game AI bots rather than volatile peer models. This paradigm shift yields five key contributions:

  • •

    Diverse game portfolio: Eight games spanning deterministic perfect-information board games (e.g., Chess, Reversi) to stochastic imperfect-information card games (e.g., Mahjong, Texas Hold’em), systematically varying determinism, information availability, and strategic complexity for comprehensive profiling of LLM reasoning capabilities.

  • •

    Anchored evaluation paradigm: Graded classic AI bots provide stable performance anchors, reducing evaluation complexity from O​(N2)O(N^{2}) to O​(N)O(N), enabling efficient model onboarding, and supporting longitudinal cross-family comparisons without benchmark recalibration.

  • •

    Balanced prompting strategy: A framework that provides necessary game information (state representation, legal actions) to ensure rule compliance while deliberately avoiding prescriptive reasoning templates, prioritizing assessment of genuine strategic decision-making over prompt engineering artifacts.

  • •

    Large-scale annotated dataset: 177,047 state-action pairs from 6,403 games, each annotated with model reasoning traces, selected actions, and environmental feedback, enabling detailed analysis of LLM decision-making, strategic adaptation, and error patterns.

  • •

    Systematic empirical analysis: Evaluation of five flagship LLMs reveals that top-performing models (notably Gemini) achieve proficiency comparable to mid-to-high-tier specialized game AI, while tests across the Qwen3 family (8B—235B parameters) confirm that strategic performance scales predictably with model size.

Beyond games, our anchored evaluation paradigm generalizes to any domain with well-defined skill hierarchies—robotic control, dialogue systems, or structured decision-making tasks. While we retain optional LLM-vs-LLM modes for supplementary analysis, our primary contribution is a scalable, reusable framework enabling rigorous longitudinal studies of strategic reasoning in LLMs222All code and data are publicly available at https://github.com/AMysteriousBeing/BotzoneBench..

2 Related Work

Our work establishes skill-graded performance anchors for LLMs in game-based evaluation. We review three lines of research: general-purpose LLM benchmarks that contextualize the static evaluation paradigm, game-based LLM benchmarks that introduce interactive assessment yet rely on relative rankings, and classic game AI platforms that offer precise skill calibration and mature competitive infrastructure.

General-purpose LLM benchmarks

LLMs have spurred numerous benchmarks measuring performance across cognitive and technical tasks in isolated, static settings. Mathematical reasoning is assessed through GSM8K Cobbe et al. (2021) and MATH Hendrycks et al. (2021), code generation via HumanEval Chen et al. (2021) and MBPP Austin et al. (2021), and broad knowledge through MMLU Hendrycks et al. (2020) and Big-Bench Hard Suzgun et al. (2023). Agentic capabilities are probed by AgentBench Liu et al. (2023) and WebArena Zhou et al. (2023b), assessing tool use and multi-step planning. However, these static paradigms suffer from two critical limitations. First, they fail to probe long-horizon strategic planning or real-time adaptive interaction. Second, they are susceptible to data leakage and test contamination Zhou et al. (2023a), where models exploit memorized patterns rather than demonstrate genuine reasoning. These inherent constraints motivate specialized game-based benchmarks for evaluating strategic decision-making.

Game-based benchmarks for LLMs

To evaluate strategic and interactive capabilities, researchers have adopted classic games as dynamic testbeds. GamaBench Huang et al. (2025) employs game-theoretic models with self-play, while GameBench Costarelli et al. (2024) expands to board and communication games with multi-agent interaction over 20-50 rounds. GameBot Lin et al. (2025) and LLMArena Chen et al. (2024) cover broader game types (board, card, arcade) and emphasize prompting techniques through LLM-vs-LLM tournaments. LVLM-Bench Wang et al. (2025) adopts visual elements with Minimax opponents, relying on completion-based metrics rather than competitive proficiency.

While these benchmarks pioneer interactive evaluation and reveal insights into spatial reasoning and opponent modeling, they share a fundamental limitation: assessments remain intrinsically relative, dependent on transient model pools, and lack stable absolute anchors for skill calibration. Furthermore, existing approaches exhibit opposing methodological extremes. GameBot heavily structures reasoning via manual Chain-of-Thought prompts, potentially constraining natural strategic thinking, while LVLM-Bench provides minimal guidance and evaluates move counts rather than play quality.

BotzoneBench addresses both limitations by (i) anchoring evaluation to skill-calibrated classic AI bots, yielding stable absolute performance metrics, and (ii) balancing information provision (game state, legal actions) with strategic autonomy, avoiding prescriptive reasoning templates while ensuring rule compliance. Performance is measured through win rates against graded AI baselines, enabling robust and interpretable assessment of strategic capability.

Classic game AI platforms

Game AI research has been supported by two complementary platform paradigms. Local simulation frameworks such as the Arcade Learning Environment Bellemare et al. (2013), OpenAI Gym (now Gymnasium Towers et al. (2024)), OpenSpiel Lanctot et al. (2019), and MineDojo Fan et al. (2022) provide developer control and reproducibility, facilitating rapid prototyping, hyperparameter tuning, and deep integration with learning frameworks. However, they generally lack an integrated ecosystem of publicly ranked agents, limiting their ability to provide stable, skill-anchored benchmarks for evaluation.

Conversely, online competitive platforms such as Botzone Zhou et al. (2018) offer diverse card and board game environments with public, stable skill hierarchies. Botzone has been widely adopted in university curricula and serves as the official platform for academic game AI competitions at IJCAI Lu and Li (2024). However, such platforms typically impose architectural constraints that hinder modern research workflows: iterative debugging requires manual upload cycles, systematic comparative evaluation lacks native support, and the integration of LLM-based agents is minimal.

BotzoneBench bridges this gap by leveraging Botzone’s stable bot hierarchies through a local development environment that combines the structured evaluation of an online platform with the flexibility of local workflows. This architecture enables efficient LLM benchmarking while maintaining compatibility with Botzone’s established skill calibration system.

3 Selected Games and Rationale

To assess the strategic reasoning and adaptive capabilities of LLMs, we selected a suite of games from the Botzone platform guided by three principles:

  • •

    Diversity in game-theoretic properties: state transition stochasticity (deterministic/stochastic), information availability (perfect/imperfect information), and agent count (2-4 players);

  • •

    Gradient in computational complexity: measured by state space size for perfect-information games, and information set count plus average information set size for imperfect-information games;

  • •

    Availability of graded classic AI baselines: essential for establishing reliable performance anchors.

The chosen games, summarized in Table 1 and detailed below, collectively form a rigorous testbed for probing LLM capabilities in interactive decision-making. Complexity measures in Table 1 are drawn from prior studies Allis (1994); Lu et al. (2023).

3.1 Deterministic, Perfect-Information Games

This category tests pure logical and strategic reasoning in fully observable environments:

  • •

    Tic-Tac-Toe: A solved game serving as a sanity check for basic rule understanding and turn-taking.

  • •

    Gomoku: A tactical pattern-matching game focused on forming five-in-a-row. Despite its vast state space, Gomoku without opening restrictions is solved with a known winning strategy Allis (1994), representing moderate strategic complexity. It tests goal-directed planning and defensive blocking.

  • •

    Ataxx: A dynamic connection/capture game where pieces move or clone. It challenges board mobility management, territorial aggression, and complex local battles.

  • •

    Reversi: A positional strategy game centered on corner control, testing long-term positional judgment and move consequence evaluation.

  • •

    Chess: The classic benchmark for strategic depth, testing planning, piece coordination, and balancing tactical combinations with strategic goals.

3.2 Stochastic, Imperfect-Information Games

This category introduces hidden information and randomness, probing probabilistic reasoning and adaptability:

  • •

    Texas Hold’em (2-Player): A canonical imperfect-information game testing probabilistic calculation (pot odds), opponent modeling (betting patterns), and strategic deception (bluffing). Each match consists of 20 hands, with victory determined by final chip count.

  • •

    Fight the Landlord: A three-player coalitional card game where two Peasants cooperate against a single Landlord. It introduces multi-agent dynamics, partial observability, and cooperative-competitive strategies, demanding flexible role adaptation and coordinated play.

  • •

    Official International Mahjong: A multi-player tile-based game characterized by imperfect information, complex scoring, and dynamic player interactions. This version follows Mahjong Competition Rules (MCR) Li et al. (2025), featuring 81 scoring patterns and requiring at least 8 points to win. It serves as a high-ceiling test for multifaceted probabilistic reasoning, memory, and adaptive tactical play under uncertainty.

Rationale

This diverse portfolio ensures our benchmark does not overfit to a single cognitive challenge. The progression from simple perfect-information to complex imperfect-information games enables us to trace how LLM reasoning capabilities scale with problem complexity in interactive environments. Crucially, each game provides a ladder of pre-calibrated classic AI bots, offering stable anchors for absolute (not merely relative) performance assessment.

We also developed LLM-based bots for Go, although they were excluded from the final benchmark due to the extreme complexity that often led to reasoning breakdowns, timeouts, or invalid decisions in late-game stages.Nevertheless, the Go environment and corresponding LLM agent have been publicly released to support future research.

4 Experimental Setup

This section details the experimental protocols for benchmarking LLM-based game-playing agents, including match design, construction and prompting of LLM bots, and selection criteria for classic AI baselines.

4.1 Evaluation Protocol: Matches and Seeding

To ensure robust and statistically meaningful comparisons, we employ a duplicate match design Quint (2007) that controls for turn-order bias and stochastic variance. Under this design, each evaluated LLM plays a duplicate set of games against every graded baseline bot, with the LLM occupying each player position (e.g., first and second mover) an equal number of times.

For perfect-information games, each LLM-baseline matchup consists of 16 duplicate games per side, totaling 32 games. For imperfect-information games, where randomness significantly affects outcomes, we increase duplicates to account for higher variance. Each matchup extends to 32 duplicate games per side, totaling 64 games.

To ensure direct comparability and full reproducibility across all evaluations, the sequence of random events (e.g., card shuffles) is deterministically controlled by a fixed set of 32 random seeds, applied identically to every bot matchup. This seeded approach eliminates luck as a confounding variable, allowing fair assessment of strategic capability.

To distinguish between transient failures (e.g., API timeouts or network connectivity issues) and genuine strategic inability, we automatically rematch any game that terminates due to such errors from the LLM-based bot. This ensures that temporary technical interruptions do not conflate with actual deficiencies in gameplay reasoning.

4.2 Architecture of LLM-Based Bot

Our evaluation integrates large language models as interactive game-playing agents. The architecture operates in a sequence that mirrors classic neural network-based agents (state observation, feature extraction, and action masking) while fundamentally differing in state representation: observations are formatted as comprehensive textual prompts rather than numerical tensors, maximizing contextual information for LLM reasoning.

At each turn, the bot receives a structured prompt containing the game state, legal actions, and, where applicable, action history. This information is processed by the LLM to produce a reasoned decision. The prompt structure is detailed in Section 4.3.

To ensure robust evaluation focused on decision-making, the architecture includes fault-tolerant handling of invalid outputs and timeouts. Each decision is allocated up to 300 seconds; a timeout results in an error, preventing unresponsiveness from being masked by automated recovery.

The LLM-based bot functions as a reasoning-driven agent that maps textual state representations to actions, enabling consistent and interpretable assessment against skill-calibrated baselines.

4.3 Prompting Strategy for LLM-Based Bot

This section details the structured prompting strategy employed to transform a LLM into a functional game-playing agent. The design follows a principled decomposition of prompts into two complementary components: a system prompt that provides static, game-agnostic instructions and constraints, and a user prompt that conveys dynamic, turn-specific information required for decision-making. This separation ensures clarity, reproducibility, and consistent reasoning across all game environments.

As illustrated in Figure 2, the system prompt comprises three core elements. The rule prompt provides a precise, textual description of the game’s rules, objectives, and key mechanics. Where necessary, concise examples clarify complex rules or common scenarios, ensuring LLMs operate with a correct understanding of the task. The encoding and information representation specifies how game states, actions, and historical sequences are formatted (e.g., grid layouts for board games, symbolic notations for cards), establishing a consistent interface between the game engine and LLMs. The output format requirement provides strict instructions on how LLMs must structure its response, including required reasoning steps and the final decision, enabling automated parsing and logging of the model’s decision process.

The user prompt, delivered at each decision step, supplies LLMs with necessary contextual information to act. Player identity information indicates which role or seat LLMs occupies in the current game. State representation provides a structured textual description of the current game state (e.g., public information, scores). For imperfect-information games, game history offers a sequential record of all publicly observable actions, designed to preserve the Markov property as fully as possible, providing LLMs with sufficient information to reason about the current belief state and infer hidden information. The valid action list enumerates all legal moves available in the current situation, constraining LLMs’ choice space and preventing invalid selections.

This two-part prompting framework balances comprehensiveness with flexibility: it provides all essential knowledge and constraints while leaving the reasoning process entirely open to the LLM’s own strategic analysis. Full prompt templates for each game are provided in Appendix C.

4.4 Selection of Graded Baseline Bots

The validity of our absolute performance metric hinges on a well-calibrated hierarchy of classic AI opponents, sourced from the extensive Botzone library. We construct this hierarchy through a selection process that integrates the Botzone Elo ladder and win-rate analysis against the immediate lower-level baseline. Level 0 (Lv0) is defined as a bot that selects a random legal move, serving as an absolute lower-bound baseline. For each candidate bot at a higher level (Lv1, Lv2, etc.), we require it to demonstrate a clear performance superiority over the preceding tier, with the win rate typically controlled between 70% and 90% in head-to-head matches. This range ensures a meaningful and distinguishable skill gap between adjacent levels: neither too narrow to compromise evaluation efficiency, nor too wide to lose discriminative power (e.g., causing all LLMs to fail uniformly against an overwhelmingly strong baseline).

To enhance the robustness and representativeness of each level, we select multiple baseline bots where the Botzone ecosystem allows. Specifically, when the public bot pool is sufficiently large, we include four bots per level; for games with moderate bot availability, two bots are chosen; and for those with limited options, one bot is used. This flexible selection strategy ensures that our benchmark remains widely applicable across games with varying community support.

Furthermore, to ensure our benchmark evaluates general strategic competence rather than specialization against a single algorithmic style, we explicitly prioritize diversity in implementation methods among bots at the same skill tier. Whenever possible, we select multiple bots that employ distinct approaches, such as rule-based, search-based, supervised learning, or reinforcement learning, yet exhibit comparable strength (e.g., similar Elo and win rates against shared baselines). This multi-bot, multi-strategy representation for a given level promotes robustness and reduces evaluation bias, providing a more comprehensive performance anchor against which LLMs can be fairly measured. Detailed descriptions of the selected baseline bots for each game and level are provided in Appendix B.

4.5 Skill Level Assessment for LLM-Based Bots

We assess each LLM-based bot by assigning it a precise skill rating anchored to our pre-calibrated hierarchy of classic AI baselines. This approach provides an absolute, interpretable measure of game-playing ability. The assessment proceeds sequentially from the bottom up. We calculate win rate as wins/(wins+losses)×100%\text{wins}/(\text{wins}+\text{losses})\times 100\%, excluding draws. Starting at level 0, a bot advances if it achieves at least 50% overall win rate against all baselines at its current level. For example, reaching 50% against Lv0 baselines qualifies it for Lv1; maintaining 50% against Lv1 baselines then advances it to Lv2, and so forth.

Once we determine the highest level a bot can reach, we express its skill as a (level, progress) tuple for all games except Tic-Tac-Toe. The level indicates the highest baseline tier the bot can match: it has beaten all lower tiers but cannot yet exceed 50% win rate against this tier’s baselines. The progress measures how far it has advanced within that tier, calculated as twice its win rate against that tier’s baselines. This doubled value represents progress toward the next tier. For instance, a bot with 34% win rate against Lv1 baselines receives (Lv1, 68%), meaning it has closed roughly two-thirds of the skill gap between Lv1 and Lv2.

Tic-Tac-Toe presents a special case. Since the game is solved and optimal play always produces draws, win rate is uninformative against perfect opponents. We therefore substitute draw rate for win rate when calculating progress.

This (level, progress) system provides an intuitive, fine-grained, and stable metric for comparing models and tracking performance improvements across training stages.

5 Experiments and Results

Due to time and budget constraints, we evaluated five flagship LLMs representing distinct model families. We tested each with default settings and unlimited output tokens, following the methodology described in Section 4. Our selection comprises DeepSeek-3.2, Claude-Sonnet-4.5, GPT-5.2, Gemini3-Pro-Preview, and Qwen3-235B-Instruct. We also evaluated smaller Qwen3 variants (7B, 14B, 32B parameters) to examine how strategic capability scales with model size. Across all models, we completed 6,403 games, collecting 177,047 state-action pairs with accompanying reasoning traces for detailed analysis.

Table 2 summarizes each model’s skill ratings across the eight games; complete results appear in Appendix A. An important caveat is that skill levels are comparable only within games, not across them. The number and strength distribution of baseline bots varies substantially between games.

Every model we tested, including the smallest (Qwen3-7B-Instruct), consistently followed game rules and produced properly formatted outputs. This confirms our prompting framework successfully enables LLMs to act as rule-compliant agents, allowing BotzoneBench to focus evaluation on strategic and adaptive capabilities.

All flagship models cleared Lv0 across every game. Among smaller models, performance tracked parameter count: Qwen3-7B-Instruct remained at Lv0 in five games, Qwen3-14B-Instruct in two, while Qwen3-32B-Instruct approached the skill level of the weakest flagship model. This scaling pattern shows that while even small LLMs can follow rules, strategic gameplay depends heavily on model capacity.

Among flagship models, Gemini3-Pro-Preview dominated most games, outperforming competitors by roughly two skill levels on average. Chess was a notable exception: GPT-5.2 led here, consistent with recent specialized studies of LLM chess understanding Wen et al. (2025). In Tic-Tac-Toe, every model matched the optimal bot, though with varying consistency. Qwen3-235B-Instruct lost only once across 32 games, while Gemini alone achieved perfect draws in every match.

7 Limitations and extensions

Our framework has two main limitations that suggest directions for future work.

First, we evaluated only five flagship models plus three smaller Qwen3 variants. We excluded Qwen3-235B-Thinking because its extended reasoning chains, which often exceed tens of thousands of tokens in late-game Mahjong, regularly triggered time limits. However, BotzoneBench’s modular design readily accommodates additional models and configurations (e.g., temperature, top-k sampling) in future studies.

Second, baseline coverage varies across games. While most games benefit from well-populated bot ecosystems with fine-grained skill ladders, Chess and Texas Hold’em face different challenges. Chess baselines show large performance gaps that reduce discrimination between adjacent skill levels. Texas Hold’em suffers from a shortage of strong public bots, limiting the upper range of our skill hierarchy. Despite these gaps, both games remain valuable for testing deterministic reasoning (Chess) and decision-making under uncertainty (Texas Hold’em). We expect baseline quality to improve as the Botzone community expands.

Beyond addressing these limitations, our framework naturally extends to studying how prompt design affects strategic reasoning. LLM performance depends heavily on prompt formulation, yet disentangling prompt engineering from inherent reasoning ability remains challenging. Our approach provides game rules and state information without prescriptive strategy hints, but many design choices remain open: level of rule detail, use of heuristics, state encoding format, and access to hidden information. Systematic manipulation of these factors could reveal how much performance stems from prompting versus underlying model capability—a question our rich interaction logs are well-suited to address.

8 Conclusion

We introduce BotzoneBench, an evaluation framework that addresses key limitations of existing LLM game-playing benchmarks. By replacing closed-pool tournaments with skill-calibrated AI baselines, we achieve three advantages: absolute performance ratings that remain valid as new models emerge, efficient linear scaling that avoids combinatorial match requirements, and rich interaction data that enables detailed analysis of strategic reasoning patterns.

Our evaluation of eight models across eight games demonstrates the framework’s ability to produce fine-grained, interpretable skill assessments while revealing distinct behavioral signatures in how models approach imperfect information and strategic uncertainty. The anchored evaluation methodology generalizes beyond games to any interactive domain with established skill hierarchies, offering a template for systematic, reusable benchmarking.

Future work should expand model coverage, strengthen baseline quality in Chess and Texas Hold’em, and systematically investigate prompt design’s role in strategic performance. The interaction logs we collect, 177,047 state-action pairs with reasoning traces, provide a foundation for such studies, enabling researchers to separate prompt engineering effects from core reasoning capabilities.

Appendix A Performance Against Baseline Anchors

This appendix provides the complete set of experimental results from which the skill ratings in Section 5 are derived. The performance of each evaluated LLM is recorded against every level of skill-calibrated baseline bots across all eight games in BotzoneBench.

The results are organized into six tables (Tables A1, A2, A3, A4, A5 and A6), each corresponding to a specific baseline level from Level 0 (random play) to Level 5. Each table cell reports the outcome of an LLM’s match against a given baseline level for a specific game, using a win-draw-loss notation: Note that w−d−l/tw-d-l/t, where ww denotes wins, dd denotes draws, ll denotes losses, and tt denotes the total number of completed games. w+d+l≤tw+d+l\leq t, as games that failed to complete after two attempts were discarded and are not counted in the totals.

The tables enable a fine-grained inspection of model performance progression. For instance, they reveal not only the highest level an LLM can reach (win rate ≥\geq50%), but also its exact win/draw/loss distribution at each intermediary level. This detailed record supports the calculation of the (Level, Progress) ratings presented in Section 5 and allows researchers to trace performance cliffs or consistency patterns (e.g., high draw rates in Chess, or volatile win rates in stochastic games).

Furthermore, these tables serve as a foundational dataset for longitudinal study. As new LLMs are evaluated or as existing models are updated, their results can be added within the same anchored framework, enabling direct and stable comparison over time. The structure ensures that performance is measured against constant, external benchmarks rather than shifting model pools, maintaining the interpretability and scalability central to the BotzoneBench paradigm.

Appendix B Baseline Bot Information

For games with sufficient available bots, we curated baseline bots up to Level 6. In Tic-Tac-Toe, due to the limited strategic depth, Level 1 bots, which consistently outperform random players, are already considered optimal. For Texas Hold’em, we curated only 3 levels due to a lack of significant performance gaps between available candidates. Similarly, for Mahjong, Fight the Landlord, and Chess, we curated 4 levels for the same reasons. Tables A9, A7, A13, A14, A10, A8, A11 and A12 provide detailed information on the baseline bots used for each game, including their ranking, score, unique BotID, average rank percentile, and average score.

AVG Rank Percentile(%) represents the percentage of all ranked bots in the Botzone platform that this baseline bot outperforms. A higher percentage indicates a stronger baseline. For example, An AVG Rank Percentile of 85% means this baseline is able to win against approximately 85% of all ranked bots in this game.
