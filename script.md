## Lead-in and Introduction

The rapid advancement of large language and vision-language models has brought a fundamental evaluation challenge to the forefront: how should we assess systems that are increasingly studied not merely as answer generators, but as agents that must act over time? While many widely used benchmarks remain valuable for testing bounded knowledge, logical reasoning, or one-shot multimodal understanding, they provide only partial evidence of the intelligence required for continuous interaction. True agentic competence emerges in closed-loop settings, where a system must track an evolving state, make decisions under uncertainty, adapt to feedback, and recover from mistakes while pursuing a goal across multiple steps. The central evaluation gap, therefore, is not simply that existing tests are too easy or too narrow, but that many of them abstract away the dynamic structure in which intelligent behavior must actually unfold.

Games provide a compelling substrate for this kind of evaluation because they preserve that dynamic structure in forms natively designed for human play. Rather than mere collections of themed tasks, games are carefully engineered challenge systems characterized by explicit rules, goals, feedback loops, state progression, and consequences that accumulate over time. These properties make games natural probes of human-relevant capability. They effectively expose a broad range of reasoning demands, including rule understanding, planning, memory, uncertainty handling, coordination, and learning from interaction, while also supporting visually grounded agent tasks in which success depends on sustained perception, action, and progress toward an objective (SmartPlay; GTBench; Cicero; Balrog). In this sense, games are valuable not because they are simply “harder” than static benchmarks, but because they retain the interactive, goal-directed, and human-oriented structure that static benchmarks frequently eliminate.

This perspective also changes how we should understand game diversity. In the context of this survey, the value of games does not lie in assembling a loose catalog of genres, but in viewing the multiverse of human games as a structured space of capability coverage. Different game structures impose different demands on reasoning, memory, coordination, adaptation, and long-horizon control. Thus, diversity matters not as surface-level variation, but as a principled mechanism for applying complementary cognitive and operational pressures on AI agents. TThis is precisely why games are unusually promising as benchmarks: they span a broad, continually expanding, and difficult-to-saturate design space. As highlighted by AI GameStore\citep{(AIGameStore)}, the point is not to identify a small set of canonical titles to solve once and for all, but to treat the multiverse of games as an extensible evaluation substrate for increasingly general, human-relevant intelligence .

However, a game environment does not automatically constitute a benchmark. It becomes one only when a protocol specifies how gameplay is presented, what qualifies as progress, and how behavior is converted into evidence. This section develops that argument through three dimensions:
- **Taxonomy** examines the types of game benchmarks the field has developed, organizing them into an evolutionary design space that spans rule-bounded settings, broader reasoning abilities, and increasingly open-ended environments.
- **Purpose** investigates what these environments are designed to measure, mapping different game structures to their motivating capability targets—ranging from rule grounding and strategic reasoning to social intelligence, visual agency, and cross-game generalization.
- **Paradigm** explores how gameplay is operationalized into benchmark evidence, analyzing the interaction interfaces and evaluation metrics that determine what a benchmark score truly represents.

Together, these three sections move from benchmark structure, to capability target, to measurement logic. This framing provides a comprehensive understanding of how existing game benchmarks are organized and how the field has evolved. It also helps clarify how benchmark design can more effectively reveal model capabilities and limitations, thereby informing future model and benchmark development.


## Taxonomy

While conventional game genres categorize titles by human player experience, benchmark taxonomies must prioritize the functional demands imposed on an AI agent. For benchmarking, the critical question is not simply what kind of game a title is, but what cognitive and operational burdens it preserves or abstracts away. Two benchmarks drawn from distant genres may serve nearly identical evaluative roles, whereas two superficially similar games may demand fundamentally different capacities for perception, memory, inference, or execution.（找一个引用） Therefore, we conceptualize the taxonomy of game benchmarks as a multidimensional design space rather than a traditional genre catalog. To systematically deconstruct these environments, our taxonomy is anchored by an evolutionary spine tracing the historical development of the field, supported by secondary coding axes—structure, scope, and modality—that describe how specific environments are instantiated. 

The evolutionary spine of this literature traces how game environments have historically advanced in tandem with foundation model capabilities, categorizing benchmark ambition across five distinct levels. Level 1 corresponds to early benchmarks built around formal, rule-grounded interaction, evaluating whether an agent can simply maintain state and make legal moves within highly controlled interfaces (e.g., SmartPlay, GTBench). Level 2 reflects a shift toward broader strategic decision-making in evolving environments, pushing the ambition from basic rule compliance to dynamic planning and interactive reasoning (e.g., PokerBench, DSGBench, BeyondScaling). Level 3 marks the integration of multi-agent social reasoning, where the evaluation target becomes the ability to cooperate, negotiate, or deceive within language-mediated arenas (e.g., Werewolf Arena, WOLF). Level 4 represents a leap toward true ecological validity, shifting the environmental form to preserve the visual and interface burdens of human play, testing whether models can operate as grounded visual agents (e.g., BALROG, StarBench). Finally, Level 5 extends the frontier from competence in a single environment to adaptability across a multiverse of games, evaluating cross-game generalization and first-contact adaptation (e.g., GameVerse, AI GAMESTORE, ARC-AGI-3). This progression highlights a continuous unified trajectory: as model architectures evolve, the game environments used to benchmark them must abstract away fewer of the native complexities of human play.

While the five-level evolutionary spine provides the historical backbone, it does not by itself specify how a benchmark instantiates the game as an evaluation environment. Benchmarks occupying the same evolutionary level often impose different operational pressures by varying along secondary coding axes.

The first secondary axis is Structure. It records how a benchmark turns gameplay into an evaluable object through two fields: Form and Construction. Form identifies the playable unit on which progress, completion, and failure are defined. Match benchmarks organize evaluation around bounded contests with clear outcomes, as in formal and strategic game suites (GTBench; GameBench). Dialogue benchmarks treat language interaction itself as the game loop, where debate, negotiation, or reference games drive the episode forward (WerewolfArena; Clembench). Encounter benchmarks focus on bounded tactical segments, while Arc benchmarks evaluate longer story or quest progressions (StarBench; FlashAdventure). World benchmarks use persistent environments where exploration, resources, tasks, or achievement graphs define progress (MCU; CivRealm). Mixed is reserved for suites that deliberately combine several of these playable units rather than fitting one dominant form (SmartPlay; BALROG; GameWorld; Orak).

Construction records the benchmark's relationship to the original game substrate. Embedded benchmarks preserve the native client or live play surface as much as possible, increasing ecological fidelity but also increasing execution noise and evaluation cost (StarBench; FlashAdventure). Wrapped benchmarks reuse existing games or environments through a shared harness, API, or sandbox, improving comparability and instrumentation while abstracting part of the native interface (SmartPlay; BALROG; GameWorld; Orak). Adapted benchmarks convert existing games, rules, or scenes into targeted evaluation tasks, such as game-theoretic settings, social-deduction arenas, or grounded rule questions (GTBench; WerewolfArena; RuleOracles). Authored benchmarks design game-like environments specifically for measurement, while Generated benchmarks make task, rule, level, or game-instance generation part of the benchmark's scalability strategy (BeyondScaling; PuzzlePlex; AIGameStore; MCU; GVGAILLM).

A separate axis is Benchmark Scope, which describes how a benchmark packages diversity to evaluate distinct operational scales. `Single game` benchmarks concentrate on one environment to facilitate deep task analysis and expert capability evaluation, as seen in Werewolf Arena, StarBench, and Beyond Scaling. \citep{WerewolfArena, StarBench, BeyondScaling}. `Game family` benchmarks expand within a relatively coherent family of related environments, avoiding overfitting and maintaining recognizable interfaces and consistent evaluation logic (eg:FlashAdventure \citep{FlashAdventure}). `Curated suites` intentionally assemble a fixed set of diverse games to evaluate a model's general competence and cover multiple capability slices under a unified protocol (e.g., SmartPlay, GameBench, DSGBench, BALROG, Orak) `Expandable suite` treat continuous growth as a core design principle, shifting the generalization focus from mastering historical game lists to handling new instances and rules generated by the platform over time (e.g. \citep{AIGameStore}). Finally, `open-ended tasks` derive their breadth from large, combinatorial, or effectively unbounded task spaces within a persistent environment (e.g., MCU, TeamCraft). It is critical to clarify that "open-ended tasks" is a scope property, not merely an environment label; it refers to the benchmark's use of long-tail task diversity rather than the mere presence of a sandbox world. 

The final axis is `Modality`, which records how the benchmark exposes observation and action channels. On the observation side, benchmarks may be text-symbolic, converting game states into natural language or structured data to maximize diagnostic clarity; visual, utilizing raw screenshots or pixel streams; or mixed, providing multi-modal inputs or offering both raw and scaffolded tracks for comparison. On the action side, benchmarks typically rely on semantic controls, where the agent outputs high-level semantic commands or API calls, or native control, requiring human-like execution such as GUI interactions or direct keyboard-mouse inputs. At the taxonomic level, modality serves to objectively document which segments of the human perception-action loop are preserved, scaffolded, or entirely abstracted away.

Taken together, these dimensions elevate the taxonomy table from a superficial genre catalog into a comprehensive design-space map. The five-level evolutionary spine traces the field's historical progression toward increasingly unconstrained generalization, while the structural, scope, and modal axes deconstruct exactly how these environments are engineered. With this architectural framework established, the next section moves a step forward to address the core objective of these environments: what specific capabilities game benchmarks actually measure, and why they serve as an unparalleled substrate for rigorous capability probes.

## Purpose

The taxonomy above organizes game benchmarks by how they instantiate playable environments. Purpose shifts from that structural map to the capability claims those environments are meant to support. The same game substrate can become a different capability probe depending on which pressure the benchmark design foregrounds: rule exposure, state dynamics, information constraints, social interdependence, perception-action burden, or task novelty. This section explains why those structures can make particular capabilities visible, and where each kind of capability claim should remain bounded.

The central question is not whether games are harder than static tasks, but how a benchmark design makes a target capability necessary for play. Capability probing depends on design choices such as how rules are exposed, how states change, how opponents or collaborators constrain action, how much perception and control burden is preserved, and how task variation prevents success from collapsing into memorized routines. These are purpose-level questions: they explain why a game can serve as a probe for an ability. 

Accordingly, each subsection below follows three guiding questions:

- Measurement target: What capability is the benchmark trying to put under pressure, and what should not be inferred from that target?
- Game affordance and boundary: Which properties of this game structure make the capability surface during play, and which aspects of real gameplay are abstracted away or only weakly tested?
- Probe design mechanisms: Which benchmark design choices make the target capability operationally necessary, such as rule-state-action formalization, information asymmetry, scenario selection, role structure, difficulty variation, scaffold contrasts, or generated task variation?

### 2.1 Level 1: Rule Understanding

At the first level, game benchmarks use games as rule-grounded formal containers. The target is not broad intelligence or expert play, but the more basic ability to enter a rule-governed action system: reading rules or manuals, mapping the current state to those rules, maintaining a valid internal state over turns, producing legal actions, and obeying the output or tool protocol through which the benchmark accepts actions. This level is therefore an entry condition for later claims. If a model cannot reliably remain inside the legal action space, avoid formatting failures, or update the state after its own moves, then lower downstream scores should not yet be interpreted as evidence of weak strategy, social reasoning, or visual agency (SmartPlay; GTBench; LLMChess).

The game affordance here is formal containment. Board games, grid games, chess-like settings, game-theoretic tasks, and tabletop rule questions have explicit rules, bounded state transitions, checkable legal actions, and automatically verifiable outcomes. That makes them cleaner than ordinary text problems for isolating rule grounding, because the benchmark can tell whether the model's proposed move is not merely plausible language but an action licensed by the game state. The boundary is equally important: many Level 1 settings expose rules, legal moves, histories, or state variables through text or APIs, thereby removing visual search, interface discovery, and low-level control. Even when visual state is reintroduced, as in screenshot-grounded board and tabletop settings, rulebook access does not guarantee situated rule application. Level 1 evidence should therefore be read as proof of controlled participation in a formal action system, not as proof of human-like play (GridBasedGameCompetitions; RuleOracles; LVLMGamePlayers).

The most important probe design mechanism is rule-state-action containment: benchmarks make rules, current state, action history, and admissible moves explicit enough that rule interpretation and legal participation become unavoidable, while still requiring the model to choose an action inside the evolving game system (SmartPlay; GTBench; BotzoneBench). A second mechanism is legal participation diagnostics, where illegal moves, malformed tool calls, invalid JSON, timeouts, and strategic losses are separated rather than collapsed into one failure label. Chess and grid-game benchmarks are especially useful here because they can distinguish legal-move generation, tool-use discipline, and move quality under exact game engines or simple simulators (LLMChess; GridBasedGameCompetitions; CompleteChessGames). The third mechanism pushes beyond memorized rules toward situated rule application: RuleOracles varies rulebook modality and asks questions about real tabletop board states, while visual board-game benchmarks ask models to parse pieces or grids before selecting valid moves. These designs show that the rule-grounding problem is not exhausted by exposing a rulebook or legal-action list; the model must bind those rules to the particular state now in front of it (RuleOracles; LVLMGamePlayers).

### 2.2 Level 2: Reasoning

Level 2 shifts the target from legal participation to interactive decision quality. The question is no longer only whether the model can make a valid move, but whether it can reason effectively when each action changes the future state, consequences are delayed, opponents or environments respond, and plans must be revised under uncertainty. This includes strategic reasoning, adaptive planning, opponent-aware decision making, symbolic and spatial planning, and time-sensitive execution. The relevant evidence is therefore trajectory-based: reasoning must survive the action-consequence loop rather than appear only as a static explanation or one-step answer (GTBench; GameBench; DSGBench; BeyondScaling).

Games are particularly suitable at this level because they externalize reasoning as play. A plan is not simply stated; it is tested through moves, feedback, resource changes, opponent responses, and accumulating mistakes. Formal games reveal incentive reasoning and hidden-information trade-offs; curated strategic suites expose a broader range of pressures such as non-determinism, cooperation, communication, and long-term planning; spatial and real-time games show whether planning can become an executable path or timed action sequence. The boundary is that much of this evidence remains textified or API-mediated. Multi-game coverage also should not be overread as transfer evidence: a suite can broaden strategic coverage without demonstrating held-out-game adaptation. Likewise, capability dimensions in DSGBench or GameBench are useful design decompositions, but they should be treated as benchmarker-defined measurement frames rather than validated natural cognitive factors (GameBench; DSGBench; KORGym).

The strongest Level 2 mechanism is formal strategic pressure: payoff structures, partial observability, repeated interaction, opponent response, solver references, or equilibrium-like anchors force the model's reasoning to appear as consequential action rather than verbal analysis. GTBench, GAMABench, TMGBench, PokerBench, and GTOWizardBenchmark instantiate this pressure at different degrees of abstraction, from canonical game-theoretic environments to poker spots or full-hand play against strong anchors, making uncertainty, action sizing, regret, and opponent modeling auditable within controlled domains (GTBench; GAMABench; TMGBench; PokerBench; GTOWizardBenchmark). A second mechanism is coverage-oriented strategic suites, where benchmark designers assemble multiple games or scenarios to cover hidden information, non-determinism, social interaction, adaptation, long-term planning, and real-time decision pressure; these suites are valuable because they prevent one narrow game from standing in for all reasoning, but their evidential claim should remain capability coverage rather than general transfer (GameBench; DSGBench; KORGym; BotzoneBench). A third mechanism is spatial-temporal execution probing: maze traversal, symbolic map planning, RTS or tower-defense settings, and matched turn-based versus real-time protocols make it visible whether a model can convert an intended plan into a sustained trajectory, avoid loops, allocate resources at the right time, and act before the environment changes. These designs show why reasoning quality cannot be separated from state maintenance, timing, and action usefulness once the benchmark becomes interactive (MazeEval; GameTraversalBenchmark; BeyondScaling; TowerMind; CivRealm).

### 2.3 Level 3: Social Intelligence

Level 3 evaluates social interdependence rather than generic social knowledge. A model succeeds only by modeling, influencing, coordinating with, or resisting other agents whose private information, incentives, utterances, and future actions matter for the outcome. The capability targets include hidden-role inference, deception production and detection, persuasion, trust calibration, theory of mind, joint planning, collaboration initiation and response, and partner-robust coordination. Social intelligence in this sense is not measured by answering questions about social situations; it is measured by whether social reasoning changes the trajectory of play (WerewolfArena; AvalonBench; LLMHanabi; CollabOvercooked).

The affordance of social games is that language, belief, commitment, and coordination become win-relevant environmental variables. In hidden-role games, a statement can change suspicion, votes, and survival; in cooperative games, a hint or request can determine whether partners coordinate on a shared plan; in negotiation games, language must align with strategic orders and future trust. The boundary is that most current social-game benchmarks are still instrumented settings. They use text-only dialogue, rules-based moderators, structured roles, fixed turn orders, LLM judges, self-labels, offline human references, or API-mediated state. They therefore provide strong evidence about instrumented social gameplay, but not a direct measurement of complete human social competence with nonverbal cues, open-ended relationships, and unconstrained social context (Cicero; Wolf; BeyondSurvival; LLMCoordination).

The central design mechanism is hidden or private information under social consequences. AvalonBench, WerewolfArena, WOLF, BeyondSurvival, and MulticulturalSpyfall make identity, role knowledge, suspicion, public dialogue, votes, and factional incentives part of the game state, so the model's deductions and deceptions have consequences for survival or victory rather than remaining detached judgments (AvalonBench; WerewolfArena; Wolf; BeyondSurvival; MulticulturalSpyfall). A second, equally important mechanism is cooperative interdependence. Hanabi, Overcooked, pure-coordination suites, and Minecraft teamwork tasks require agents to infer partners' rationales, share limited information, initiate help, respond to requests, allocate work, and adapt to changing joint state; these benchmarks show that collaboration is not merely friendly language but a requirement to complete the task (LLMHanabi; StrategicHanabi; CollabOvercooked; LLMCoordination; TeamCraft). Negotiation and influence form a third mechanism: Diplomacy-style play shows how promises, coalition formation, persuasion, and betrayal risk can couple natural-language interaction to strategic execution, although such ecological human-play evidence is harder to standardize than simulated benchmarks (Cicero; WerewolfArena; AvalonBench). Finally, process decomposition makes social success analyzable: deception-type labels, suspicion trajectories, CoordQA, initiating/responding collaboration metrics, and role-conditioned outcomes help explain why a social agent succeeds or fails without reducing social intelligence to a single win rate (Wolf; LLMCoordination; CollabOvercooked; BeyondSurvival).

### 2.4 Level 4: Visual Agency

Level 4 targets the knowing-doing gap: whether a model can turn visual or semi-visual game states into effective actions over time. The capability is not perception alone, nor planning alone, but the coupled ability to ground a screen, identify actionable objects and UI affordances, localize actions, manage timing, preserve memory, recover from errors, and keep advancing toward a goal. A model may describe the right objective or strategy and still fail if it clicks the wrong region, misreads readiness text, becomes trapped against geometry, repeats stale actions, or loses track of quest progress (StarBench; Balrog; PokeGym).

Visual games are suitable because they preserve the screen-to-action loop faced by human players. The agent must observe a dynamic surface, infer what matters, decide what to do, and execute the action through a control channel whose mistakes affect later state. This makes static visual competence insufficient: object recognition, temporal grounding, spatial localization, UI control, and recovery become behaviorally visible. The boundary is that greater ecological fidelity also entangles failure modes. Low scores in raw visual environments may reflect perceptual hallucination, UI grounding failure, latency, invalid action formatting, poor planning, memory collapse, or inability to recover from minor control errors. Level 4 should therefore not be framed as "more realistic is automatically better," but as a capability layer where interface privilege determines what the benchmark can claim (VideoGameBench; FlashAdventure; GameWorld).

The first mechanism is perception-before-control probing. GameplayQA, LVLMGamePlayers, ING-VP, Atari-GPT, V-MAGE, and RuleOracles isolate visual grounding, board parsing, spatial relations, temporal attribution, or screenshot-based rule application before full control is introduced, showing that visual game understanding is already difficult even without long-horizon play (GameplayQA; LVLMGamePlayers; INGVP; AtariGPT; VMage; RuleOracles). The most diagnostically powerful mechanism, however, is matched perception-action contrast: StarBench keeps the same RPG tasks and native metrics while comparing raw direct control with semantic tool-assisted control and OCR/detector assistance; GameWorld compares computer-use agents with deterministic semantic actions under a shared browser runtime; LMGameBench and Balrog expose how harnesses, wrappers, text descriptions, memory, or optional images change apparent competence; VideoGameBench and TowerMind further show how paused, real-time, text-state, and raw-pixel variants alter the meaning of performance (StarBench; GameWorld; LMGameBench; Balrog; VideoGameBench; TowerMind). A third mechanism is progress and recovery probing, where full story arcs, quest milestones, checkpoint progress, state-verifiable tasks, deadlock categories, and milestone judges make sustained visual agency visible beyond a single good action. FlashAdventure foregrounds clue memory and full story completion, PokeGym isolates deadlock and physical recovery in a 3D quest world, and GameWorld, VideoGameBench, Balrog, and Crafter show that progress, exploration, and recovery often remain weak even when the interaction loop is structured enough to score automatically (FlashAdventure; PokeGym; GameWorld; VideoGameBench; Balrog; Crafter).

### 2.5 Level 5: Cross-Game Generalization and Open-Ended Task Generalization

Level 5 should not be equated with "many games." Its target is whether an agent can preserve useful game competence beyond a fixed, known task: across new games, new rules, new levels, new task combinations, hidden mechanics, or first-contact environments. This includes cross-genre robustness, benchmark-space growth, intra-world open-ended task generalization, generated rule variation, and novelty-protected adaptation. The capability claim is therefore stronger than broad evaluation coverage, and it requires careful wording about what kind of generalization each benchmark actually supports (AIGameStore; Orak; ARCAGI3).

Games are suitable for this purpose because human game spaces are naturally extensible. A single world can generate long-tail tasks, a game family can vary rules or levels, a curated suite can combine heterogeneous mechanics, and a living platform can continue sourcing or generating new games. This makes games promising for anti-saturation and generalist-agent evaluation. The boundary is that these are different kinds of evidence. Fixed curated suites such as Orak, GameVerse, LMGameBench, TextQuests, TextAtari, and TextArena mainly show breadth, robustness across game forms, or scaffold behavior across a known collection. Minecraft, Stardew Valley, Crafter, NetHack, and related environments show open-ended task diversity inside one world, not cross-title transfer. Generated, private, or first-contact settings are closer to novelty and anti-saturation, but they still vary in whether the novelty lies in rules, levels, task configurations, or whole games (Orak; GameVerse; TextQuests; MCU; StarDojo; NetHackLearningEnvironment).

The first mechanism is single-world open-ended task construction, where a persistent game world supports many goals, resource chains, task variants, and long-horizon dependencies. MCU scales Minecraft tasks through atomic and compositional task generation, StarDojo expands production-living objectives inside Stardew Valley, TeamCraft varies collaborative Minecraft goals, scenes, and agent counts, MineNPC-Task uses player-authored Minecraft requests with validator-backed repair, and Crafter or NetHack show the historical value of broad achievement or procedural challenge inside one hard world. These benchmarks are important because they test intra-world generalization, memory, repair, exploration, and task composition, but they should not be cited as cross-game transfer evidence (MCU; StarDojo; TeamCraft; MineNPCTask; Crafter; NetHackLearningEnvironment). The second mechanism is broad curated-suite probing: Orak, GameVerse, LMGameBench, TextQuests, TextAtari, TextArena, and GameWorld assemble multiple games or genres to test whether models and scaffolds remain useful across different game forms. Their value lies in coverage and robustness, especially when they expose interface, reflection, memory, or leaderboard effects, but their fixed composition means they do not by themselves demonstrate first-contact transfer to unseen games (Orak; GameVerse; LMGameBench; TextQuests; TextAtari; TextArena; GameWorld). The strongest Level 5 mechanism is generated, living, or first-contact novelty: GVGAILLM uses the extensible GVGAI/VGDL space to make symbolic games and levels scalable; AIGameStore treats the multiverse of human games as an expandable benchmark object with public/private splits and human-relative scoring; PuzzleJAX turns a large PuzzleScript ecosystem into a validated symbolic game family; Mars perturbs world rules so pretrained commonsense can become misleading; and ARCAGI3 asks agents to infer hidden mechanics and goals on novel interactive environments under human-normalized efficiency. These designs make novelty, anti-saturation, mechanic induction, and first-contact adaptation central rather than incidental (GVGAILLM; AIGameStore; PuzzleJAX; Mars; ARCAGI3).


## Paradigm: From Interaction to Evaluation

A game environment does not automatically constitute a benchmark merely by being interactive or difficult. It becomes an evaluation instrument only when gameplay is mediated by an interaction contract and an evaluation contract. The former determines what part of the human play loop is exposed to the model, while the latter determines what kind of evidence can be extracted from the resulting behavior. In this sense, the methodological challenge of game benchmarking is not simply to select harder games, but to transform gameplay into a measurement pipeline that is interpretable, comparable, and sustainable as model capabilities evolve.

### Interaction

Interface design operates as the front end of this evaluation pipeline. It specifies what the model is allowed to see, how it is allowed to act, and which cognitive or operational burdens of human play are preserved, simplified, or removed. Consequently, two benchmarks may use similar game substrates but probe fundamentally different capabilities if one presents a symbolic state and semantic actions while the other requires visual grounding and native control. In this context, interaction design should be treated as a core part of benchmark validity rather than as an implementation detail.

**Observation** channels trace one of the clearest paradigm shifts in game benchmarks: the transition from abstract textual or symbolic state representations to raw visual streams. Textified interfaces align naturally with language-centric LLMs and excel in reasoning-oriented evaluations.  By explicitly exposing rules, state variables, histories, and action constraints, they support highly controlled diagnostic probing (SmartPlay; GTBench; DSGBench; Sudoku Bench, OpenGuanDan). However, scaling such interfaces to complex visual or commercial games necessitates bespoke APIs, wrappers, or state-extraction procedures. This fundamentally bypasses the challenge of inferring game states directly from the play surface, representing an agent evaluation that remains relatively decoupled from human-like patterns.

Therefore, the shift toward raw visual input is not simply a change in modality; it is a commitment to more human-like, end-to-end agent evaluation. Models are forced to interpret screen pixels, track dynamic visual changes, and make decisions under severe perceptual uncertainty. Unsurprisingly, current systems struggle in these settings, as raw visual observations reintroduce the state noise, grounding ambiguity, and temporal instability that text abstractions had neatly excised (VideoGameBench; PokeGym; GameplayQA). To bridge this gap, hybrid designs—such as OCR-assisted, detector-assisted, or dual text-and-image tracks—occupy a critical middle ground. They recognize that while pure vision best approximates human play, providing intermediate perceptual scaffolding yields a more diagnostic benchmark, isolating higher-order reasoning failures from low-level perceptual bottlenecks (StarBench; Orak).

**Action** channels determine how a model's decisions manifest within the game environment. Semantic actions regularize the agent's output space by restricting choices to high-level moves, skills, targets, or predefined commands that are seamlessly parsed and executed by the benchmark harness (SmartPlay; GTBench; DSGBench; Orak).  By eliminating the friction of motor execution and UI manipulation, this design directs evaluation toward pure reasoning, planning, and decision quality. 

Native control, by contrast, requires the model to interact through interfaces originally designed for human players, such as mouse, keyboard, controller, or GUI actions. This elevates the agentic requirements of the task: the model must not only formulate a strategy but also ground that strategy in executable, low-level interactions with the game's surface.Furthermore, native-control interfaces provide a scalable pathway for evaluating commercial and browser games. By leveraging standardized, human-facing control channels, researchers can bypass the prohibitive engineering cost of developing custom symbolic APIs for every new game environment(FlashAdventure; VideoGameBench; GameWorld; StarBench).

The intersection of these observation and action choices establishes an interface privilege hierarchy, underscoring why benchmark results must be interpreted with caution. Highly privileged textified and API-mediated environments strip away the burdens of perception, UI grounding, and precise action execution. While this yields stable, automatically verifiable instruments ideal for isolating high-level strategic reasoning, it fundamentally weakens claims about whether a model can understand and act within an unconstrained environment as presented to humans.

Low-privilege, human-like interfaces preserve the visual grounding, interface comprehension, and temporal coordination essential for end-to-end agent evaluation. However, this ecological validity comes at the cost of entangled failure modes. In a native visual environment, a low score could stem from flawed planning, but it is equally likely to result from perceptual hallucinations, latency mismatches, invalid control formatting, or the inability to recover from a minor UI misclick (VideoGameBench; PokeGym; StarBench; GameWorld). Thus, interface privilege should not be treated a conscious design variable that delineates the specific capabilities a benchmark can validly claim to measure. A rigorous game benchmark must explicitly document its privilege level and, ideally, provide comparative tracks between assisted and human-like settings to successfully disentangle reasoning competence from grounding and execution failures (GameVerse).

### Evaluation

The evaluation contract defines what can be validly inferred from gameplay. If the interaction contract determines what kind of game loop the model faces, the evaluation contract determines how agent behavior is synthesized into evidence. Games naturally contain goals, rewards, progress, and failure states, but these native signals do not automatically constitute a rigorous benchmark. A game becomes a benchmark only when its outcomes are translated into metrics that are interpretable, comparable, and robust against the noise of stochastic interactions. 

The most direct paradigm is **result-based evaluation**. Games provide native outcome signals such as win rate, score, reward, progress, survival, or task completion, making this paradigm highly natural to assess end-to-end agentic performance. It preserves the original objective structure of the game and is usually easy to automate, which makes it attractive for large-scale benchmark construction. Many game benchmarks rely on this logic because final outcomes remain the clearest evidence of whether an agent can actually achieve the goal of play (SmartPlay; GTBench; Balrog; GameWorld).

However, result-based metrics are often "black-box" indicators that lack diagnostic details. A low score in a game typically conflates multiple failure modes ranging from poor planning, weak rule understanding, visual misrecognition, invalid action, latency or memory collapse. This problem becomes especially severe in visually grounded or long-horizon games, where current models often achieve extremely low native progress and the metric loses diagnostic resolution (VideoGameBench; StarBench). Furthermore, native scores suffer from weak semantic portability: a score in one game rarely rarely maps to the same score in another, complicating the aggregation of results in multi-game suites.

This motivates a second paradigm: **process-level and diagnostic** evaluation. These metrics decompose gameplay into intermediate milestones, trajectory analysis, sub-skill scores, and reasoning checks. By tracking "how" an agent behaves along the path to a goal, these instruments remain informative even when final success is sparse. This is particularly important for long-horizon and open-ended games, where binary completion hides most of the agent's behavior. Benchmarks that add intermediate reasoning or capability-specific metrics show that models with similar outcomes can differ substantially in the quality of their decisions, state tracking, or recovery behavior (GAMEBoT; DSGBench; GameWorld; MCU; GameVerse).

The strength of process-level evaluation is interpretability, but this paradigm introduces design dependency. Once a benchmark decomposes gameplay into subskills or milestones, the decomposition itself reflects the benchmark creator’s prior assumptions about positive behavior. For instance, PuzzleJAX(PuzzleJAX)utilizes the per-level win/completion rates of a subset of games as procedural metrics. GameVerse(GameVerse) adopts a VLM-based and manually verificated milestone-based scoring system. 
While the milestone-based evaluation of the latter offers greater generalizability than the former, it remains evident that fundamental conceptual differences exist in the metric designs across varying game genres. Therefore, while these metrics improve failure analysis, they trade off some of the objectivity. They are most useful when treated as an explanatory layer around outcome metrics, rather than as a universal replacement for them.

A third paradigm, **Adversarial and Relative Evaluation** evaluates performance dynamically through adversarial interaction or multi-agent social networks. Unlike static test sets, adversarial environments resist rapid saturation because the benchmark's difficulty scales in tandem with the capabilities of the opposing agents. This dynamic pressure is uniquely suited for probing opponent modeling, deceptive negotiation and cooperative strategy capabilities that strictly emerge through live interaction (GTBench; WerewolfArena; GameArena). Consequently, adversarial frameworks maintain benchmark freshness and capture dimensions of social intelligence that isolated, single-agent tasks inherently miss. However, arena-style evaluations fundamentally alter the semantics of a score. Results derived from tournaments are inherently relative: they quantify how a model performs against a specific opponent pool under a fixed protocol, but they do not isolate the absolute source of that capability. Rankings can fluctuate wildly if the opponent pool, prompting strategy, or match structure is modified.

Calibration is therefore necessary for giving game scores external meaning. A calibrated benchmark does not simply report a 60% win rate or an arbitrary numerical score; it articulates exactly what that proficiency represents against a known, stable baseline. By introducing fixed AI anchors, explicitly tiered opponent ladders, or calibrated solver references, benchmark designers can transform fluctuating relative performances into interpretable claims. Human baselines, expert anchors, solver references, random baselines, novice/expert comparisons, or human-normalized efficiency are critical for grounding model performance.(SmartPlay; BotzoneBench; ARCAGI3; GameWorld). This ensures that the same numerical score is not overinterpreted across different games or varying interface privileges within a suite. 

Robustness is the complementary requirement. A benchmark score must not only be interpretable but also remain stable and meaningful under reasonable variations in the testing environment. Beyond statistical stability, robustness demands that the benchmark actively defends against the broader systemic issues of dataset contamination and benchmark-specific optimization. To address this, recent rigorous benchmarks have begun implementing explicit defensive mechanisms. These include utilizing private explicit by using private or dynamically held-out evaluation content instituting strict contamination checks, enforcing scaffold-ablated comparisons, and maintaining a clear separation between public development environments and official, closed-door leaderboard evaluations (ARCAGI3; LMGAMEBench; Clembench2024; GameWorld). These practices show that evaluation design must defend not only against statistical noise, but also against the broader problem that rapidly improving agents may optimize for the benchmark protocol rather than the underlying capability.

Ultimately, evaluation within game environments must be approached as a pluralistic measurement system rather than a monolithic scoreboard. Result-based metrics honor the native objective of the game; process-level diagnostics explain the mechanics of behavior; adversarial protocols introduce dynamic stress tests; calibration applies external semantics; and robustness preserves those semantics against optimization drift. The most rigorous game benchmarks do not merely select difficult games; they transparently articulate what their scores represent, what blind spots remain, and how their protocols will sustain validity as the frontier of agentic intelligence advances.
