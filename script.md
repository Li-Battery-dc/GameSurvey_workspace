# Writing Scripts

## Lead-in and Introduction

The rapid advancement of large language and vision-language models has brought a fundamental evaluation challenge to the forefront: how should we assess systems that are increasingly studied not merely as answer generators, but as agents that must act over time? While many widely used benchmarks remain valuable for testing bounded knowledge, logical reasoning, or one-shot multimodal understanding, they provide only partial evidence of the intelligence required for continuous interaction. True agentic competence emerges in closed-loop settings, where a system must track an evolving state, make decisions under uncertainty, adapt to feedback, and recover from mistakes while pursuing a goal across multiple steps. The central evaluation gap, therefore, is not simply that existing tests are too easy or too narrow, but that many of them abstract away the dynamic structure in which intelligent behavior must actually unfold.

Games provide a compelling substrate for this kind of evaluation because they preserve that dynamic structure in forms natively designed for human play. Rather than mere collections of themed tasks, games are carefully engineered challenge systems characterized by explicit rules, goals, feedback loops, state progression, and consequences that accumulate over time. These properties make games natural probes of human-relevant capability. They effectively expose a broad range of reasoning demands, including rule understanding, planning, memory, uncertainty handling, coordination, and learning from interaction, while also supporting visually grounded agent tasks in which success depends on sustained perception, action, and progress toward an objective (SmartPlay; GTBench; HumanLevelDiplomacy; Balrog). In this sense, games are valuable not because they are simply “harder” than static benchmarks, but because they retain the interactive, goal-directed, and human-oriented structure that static benchmarks frequently eliminate.

This perspective also changes how we should understand game diversity. In the context of this survey, the value of games does not lie in assembling a loose catalog of genres, but in viewing the multiverse of human games as a structured space of capability coverage. Different game structures impose different demands on reasoning, memory, coordination, adaptation, and long-horizon control. Thus, diversity matters not as surface-level variation, but as a principled mechanism for applying complementary cognitive and operational pressures on AI agents. TThis is precisely why games are unusually promising as benchmarks: they span a broad, continually expanding, and difficult-to-saturate design space. As highlighted by AI GameStore\citep{(AIGameStore)}, the point is not to identify a small set of canonical titles to solve once and for all, but to treat the multiverse of games as an extensible evaluation substrate for increasingly general, human-relevant intelligence .

However, a game environment does not automatically constitute a benchmark. It becomes one only when a protocol specifies how gameplay is presented, what qualifies as progress, and how behavior is converted into evidence. This section develops that argument through three dimensions:
- **Taxonomy** examines the types of game benchmarks the field has developed, organizing them into an evolutionary design space that spans rule-bounded settings, broader reasoning abilities, and increasingly open-ended environments.
- **Purpose** investigates what these environments are designed to measure, mapping different game structures to their motivating capability targets—ranging from rule grounding and strategic reasoning to social intelligence, visual agency, and cross-game generalization.
- **Paradigm** explores how gameplay is operationalized into benchmark evidence, analyzing the interaction interfaces and evaluation metrics that determine what a benchmark score truly represents.
  
Together, these three sections move from benchmark structure, to capability target, to measurement logic. This framing provides a comprehensive understanding of how existing game benchmarks are organized and how the field has evolved. It also helps clarify how benchmark design can more effectively reveal model capabilities and limitations, thereby informing future model and benchmark development.


## Taxonomy

While conventional game genres categorize titles by human player experience, benchmark taxonomies must prioritize the functional demands imposed on an AI agent. For benchmarking, the critical question is not simply what kind of game a title is, but what cognitive and operational burdens it preserves or abstracts away. Two benchmarks drawn from distant genres may serve nearly identical evaluative roles, whereas two superficially similar games may demand fundamentally different capacities for perception, memory, inference, or execution. Therefore, we conceptualize the taxonomy of game benchmarks as a multidimensional design space rather than a traditional genre catalog. To systematically deconstruct these environments, the taxonomy (table) is organized around five complementary dimensions: benchmark level, structure, benchmark scope, and modality. Together, these dimensions describe both the historical evolution of the literature and the concrete ways in which different benchmarks operationalize game-based evaluation \citep{SmartPlay, GTBench, Orak, AIGameStore}.

The evolutionary spine of game benchmarks for LFMs in this survey categorizes benchmarks across five distinct levels of capability. Level 1 benchmarks use games as rule-grounded formal containers to measure an agent's foundational ability to generate legal actions and maintain state within highly controlled interfaces \citep{GTBench, SmartPlay, BotzoneBench}. Level 2 elevates the challenge from rule compliance to interactive strategic reasoning. While some benchmarks at this stage rely on analytically clean, game-theoretic probes with parameterized incentives (e.g., GAMABench, GTBench), others broaden into multi-game suites designed to evaluate hidden information processing and dynamic adaptation (e.g., GameBench, DSGBench). Crucially, this level introduces execution pressure: environments such as Beyond Scaling and CivRealm demonstrate that strategic competence cannot be reduced to turn-based deliberation, as model rankings shift sharply once real-time pressure, latency, or expanding world complexity are incorporated \citep{BeyondScaling, CivRealm}. Level 3 positions social intelligence as the primary evaluation target, branching into two distinct paradigms. The adversarial, language-mediated branch centers on hidden roles, persuasion, negotiation, and betrayal (e.g., Werewolf Arena, HumanLevelDiplomacy), whereas the cooperative branch emphasizes coordination, partner modeling, rationale inference, and the ability to initiate collaboration under shared goals (e.g., CollabOvercooked, LLMCoordination, LLMHanabi, StrategicHanabi, TeamCraft). Level 4 marks the shift toward visual agency, grounding evaluation in ecologically rich play where perception and interface execution are preserved rather than abstracted away \citep{Balrog, StarBench, FlashAdventure}. Finally, Level 5 pushes the boundary to cross-game generalization and first-contact adaptability, testing whether agents can sustain competence across expanding game universes or entirely novel mechanics without overfitting to a fixed title (e.g., Orak, GameVerse, AI GAMESTORE).

While this five-level evolutionary spine provides the primary narrative arc for understanding benchmark development, this macroscopic hierarchy alone cannot capture the full complexity of the design space. Benchmarks sitting at the exact same evolutionary level can differ radically in how they organize formal interactions, shape the environmental substrate, and bound the perception-action loop. To systematically deconstruct these microscopic differences, our taxonomy must move beyond the overarching evolutionary spine and dissect the environments through three orthogonal design dimensions: structure, scope, and modality.

We therefore use a two-part structural description description: Game Structure and Environment Structure. isolates the formal mechanisms: whether information is perfect or imperfect, transitions are deterministic or stochastic, play is single-agent or multi-agent, social incentives are cooperative, competitive, or mixed, and interactions unfold in turn-based or real-time regimes. Environment Structure, by contrast, captures the family of game environments in which those mechanics are instantiated: tabletop settings, abstract puzzles, social interaction arenas, combat-strategy worlds, adventure-quest worlds, and sandbox-open-world environments. These two layers share a joint idea that researchers choose games as benchmarks because this structural intersection dictates the exact agentic challenge. A cooperative imperfect-information card game \citep(Hanabi) and an adversarial hidden-role arena \citep(Werewolf) both necessitate multi-agent reasoning, but they distribute uncertainty and communicative burdens in entirely different ways. Historically, the design space has widened along both halves of this structural axis. Early benchmark design was easier to control in formal game-theoretic settings, board-like worlds, and compact symbolic environments \citep{GTBench, GAMABench}. Historically, as the literature evolved from analytically clean, formal game-theoretic settings and compact symbolic environments toward board games, social arenas, and complex sandbox worlds, researchers gained unprecedented freedom to manipulate information regimes, timing constraints, and task horizons, thereby generating a much broader evaluation space than genre labels alone would suggest.

A separate axis is Benchmark Scope, which describes how a benchmark packages diversity to evaluate distinct operational scales. `Single game` benchmarks concentrate on one environment to facilitate deep task analysis and expert capability evaluation, as seen in Werewolf Arena, StarBench, and Beyond Scaling. \citep{WerewolfArena, StarBench, BeyondScaling}. `Game family` benchmarks expand within a relatively coherent family of related environments, avoiding overfitting and maintaining recognizable interfaces and consistent evaluation logic (eg:FlashAdventure \citep{FlashAdventure}). `Curated suites` intentionally assemble a fixed set of diverse games to evaluate a model's general competence and cover multiple capability slices under a unified protocol (e.g., SmartPlay, GameBench, DSGBench, BALROG, Orak) `Expandable suite` treat continuous growth as a core design principle, shifting the generalization focus from mastering historical game lists to handling new instances and rules generated by the platform over time (e.g. \citep{AIGameStore}). Finally, `open-ended tasks` derive their breadth from large, combinatorial, or effectively unbounded task spaces within a persistent environment (e.g., MCU, TeamCraft). It is critical to clarify that "open-ended tasks" is a scope property, not merely an environment label; it refers to the benchmark's use of long-tail task diversity rather than the mere presence of a sandbox world. 

The final axis is `Modality`, which records how the benchmark exposes observation and action channels. On the observation side, benchmarks may be text-symbolic, converting game states into natural language or structured data to maximize diagnostic clarity; visual, utilizing raw screenshots or pixel streams; or mixed, providing multi-modal inputs or offering both raw and scaffolded tracks for comparison. On the action side, benchmarks typically rely on semantic controls, where the agent outputs high-level semantic commands or API calls, or native control, requiring human-like execution such as GUI interactions or direct keyboard-mouse inputs. At the taxonomic level, modality serves to objectively document which segments of the human perception-action loop are preserved, scaffolded, or entirely abstracted away.

Taken together, these dimensions elevate the taxonomy table from a superficial genre catalog into a comprehensive design-space map. The five-level evolutionary spine traces the field's progression from rule-grounded formal evaluation to broad generalization challenges, while the structural, scope, and modal axes deconstruct precisely how diverse agent challenges are operationalized. With this architectural framework established, the next section moves a step forward to address the core objective of these environments: what specific capabilities game benchmarks actually measure, and why they serve as an unparalleled substrate for rigorous capability probes.


## Purpose

The evolution of this literature reflects a shifting ambition in game-based evaluation. Games have transitioned from controlled, formal containers for diagnosing bounded reasoning skills \citep{SmartPlay, GTBench} into socially and perceptually rich environments testing grounded control\citep{WerewolfArena, Balrog, StarBench}, with boundary cases like ARC-AGI-3 emphasizing first-contact adaptation to novel hidden-mechanic environments and benchmark-design robustness \citep{ARCAGI3}, and broader game universes demanding generalist adaptability \citep{aigamescore57}. The following five levels unpack this progression, complemented by a design-space framework that compares benchmarks across structural, modal, and level-target dimensions.

**Level 1: Rule Understanding - Games as rule-grounded formal containers**  Benchmarks for Large Foundation Models (LFMs) inherit their logic from classical reinforcement learning, in which the primary value of games lies in providing a rule-governed, machine-checkable interaction loop rather than rich ecological realism or deep strategic breadth. Earlier game benchmarks were already valued for exactly this property: they exposed explicit state transitions, constrained action spaces, and objective feedback under standardized interfaces, making games a practical substrate for evaluating whether an agent could reliably operate within a formal environment (NetHackLearningEnvironment). Recent LLM benchmarks extend this focusing on agentic rule-grounding: before asking whether a model can reason strategically, they first ask whether it can understand the rules, maintain the game state, and continue producing legal actions inside a controlled interactive container (SmartPlay; GTBench). In practice, this level was dominated by small board or grid games and game-theoretic frameworks with heavily normalized text or symbolic interfaces. Here, performance depended as much on instruction following, state maintenance, and output conformity as on strategic depth; even simple changes in board representation could sharply increase invalid moves, while many failures in more formal setups reflected action-format or tool-use errors before strategic weakness could even be cleanly assessed (GridBasedGameCompetitions; LLMChess; RuleOracles). Precisely for this reason, later work increasingly treated rule understanding not as a trivial prerequisite but as part of the reasoning challenge itself: once legality and state fidelity are unstable, higher-level strategic evaluation becomes difficult to interpret, motivating the shift from rule-grounded participation to strategy-grounded play.

**Level 2: Stagetic reasoning - Game as interactive reasoning environment and diagnostic probes** Game benchmarks signify the transition from static to interactive reasoning. The core evaluation is no longer simply whether a model can operate within a legal game loop, but whether it can sustain sound decisions as system states evolve, information remains incomplete, opponents adapt, and consequences unfold over time. Early research at this level inherits the analytical discipline of formal game-theoretic evaluation, utilizing strictly defined strategic settings to measure decision quality through explicit incentives and exact scoring rather than unconstrained ecological interaction (GTBench; GAMABench; TMGBench). Nevertheless, the literature rapidly expands from isolated formal probes to multi-game diagnostic frameworks, where game diversity represents comprehensive capability coverage rather than mere surface variation. Distinct games impose different reasoning burdens, transforming the benchmark into a mechanism for mapping strategic competence across uncertainty management, opponent modeling, long-horizon planning, and adaptive decision-making (GameBench; DSGBench). Crucially, this level also bridges an evaluation gap rarely addressed by conventional language benchmarks, yet vital for VLM-style agents: spatial and physical reasoning embedded within interactive loops. Instead of treating physics or spatial understanding as isolated question-answering exercises, game-like environments evaluate whether models can translate observations into effective actions under structured but consequential dynamics. DeepPHY executes this for action-grounded physical reasoning, whereas MazeEval and GameTraversalBenchmark illustrate that route planning, spatial state maintenance, and failure modes (e.g., looping or near-correct traversal) can be directly evaluated in sequential settings. More complex strategic environments further advance this logic: CivRealm demonstrates that interactive reasoning must withstand delayed consequences, dynamically expanding state and action spaces, and the disparity between decomposed subtasks and full-world competence. Conversely, BeyondScaling highlights that reasoning and execution quality can decouple significantly when turn-based mechanics are replaced by real-time pressure. Specialized and ecological edge cases delineate the boundaries of this level without dominating its core: PokerBench distills hidden-information strategy into a solver-calibrated benchmark, while HumanLevelDiplomacy serves as a persistent reminder that language-mediated coordination functions as a strategically consequential element within a single game. Taken together, these benchmarks establish Level 2 as the stage where games function as diagnostic probes of interactive decision quality, advancing evaluation from basic rule compliance to strategy, spatial reasoning, physics, and action execution under dynamic conditions.

## Pradigm

### Interfaces

Games are an exceptionally strong substrate for benchmark design primarily because they provide native, human-facing interaction channels—such as raw pixel streams, complex GUI layouts, and human-like input controls. Driven by the recent mainstream shift toward true ecological evaluation, operating directly on these raw interfaces has become the gold standard for assessing genuine agency. However, because end-to-end visual-motor grounding remains highly challenging, benchmark designers often face a fundamental trade-off: to ensure tractability, environments are frequently abstracted through semantic privilege, such as structured state APIs, simplified simulators, or auxiliary textification tools (StarBench; Orak). While these abstracted channels offer necessary convenience, they represent a compromise that masks the underlying complexity of real-world interaction, often shielding models from the catastrophic performance drops observed when forced to act without such privileged assistance (StarBench).

### Evaluation

Conversely, while APIs are a compromise for interaction, they are an unequivocal advantage for evaluation. The software accessibility of digital games enables powerful, automated assessment on the backend. Frameworks can utilize evaluator-only API bridges to compute state-verifiable progress (GameWorld) or employ memory scanning to verify quest completion (PokeGym), all without leaking privileged state information to the agent. Ultimately, recent work suggests that the benchmarking paradigm is moving toward a highly decoupled, atomic contract: preserving the rigorous, human-like observation-action interface on the front end, while exploiting the open operability of game engines for robust, automated verification on the back end (GameWorld; LMGameBench; ARCAGI3).
