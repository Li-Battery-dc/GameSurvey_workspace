## Lead-in and Introduction

While static QA tests evaluate isolated cognitive skills, they fundamentally fail to capture continuous agentic behaviors like long-horizon planning and adaptive error recovery. The central value of games lies in their ability to provide explicit rules, sequential interaction, and auditable progress signals across diverse domains. They allow for the controlled diagnosis of rule-following in formal settings (SmartPlay; GTBench; BotzoneBench; Clembench; ThirdParadigm), test binding behavioral commitments like deception and cooperation in social arenas (WerewolfArena; HumanLevelDiplomacy; TextArena), and crucially, reconnect perception to control in visually grounded worlds (Balrog; VideoGameBench; GameWorld). This makes game environments indispensable for evaluating dynamic failures—such as affordance detection and state tracking—driving the field's transition toward genuine agency.

To fully grasp the evaluation potential of game benchmarks, we must recognize the human game domain as a culturally evolved space uniquely calibrated to human intelligence. Rather than viewing game diversity as a superficial taxonomy, we argue that it serves as a comprehensive suite of capability probes. Games naturally decompose complex, human-like intelligence into manageable interactive loops, offering built-in tests for a vast spectrum of abilities—from rigorous rule-grounding to dynamic, open-world autonomy. This diversity provides empirical evidence that game environments are not just harder testbeds, but naturally structured carriers for the very capabilities required by genuine AGI.

This intrinsic alignment makes game benchmarks an exceptionally suitable and human-calibrated medium for Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) evaluation. The field is increasingly recognizing that genuine AGI requires environments that test dynamic problem-solving and first-contact adaptation(ARC-AGI-3). Furthermore, the vast structural diversity of human games should be harnessed as a dense resource for capability coverage, rather than treated as mere environmental noise(AIGameStore). Consequently, the central focus of benchmarking shifts from merely asking whether models can play games, to precisely what agentic dimensions different game structures inherently reveal. This necessitates a purposeful analysis of games as targeted capability probes, which we explore in the subsequent sections.

Games are an exceptionally strong substrate for benchmark design primarily because they provide native, human-facing interaction channels—such as raw pixel streams, GUI layouts, and keyboard-and-mouse controls. Driven by the recent mainstream shift toward true human-like evaluation, operating directly on these raw interfaces has become the gold standard for ecological validity. However, because pure visual-motor control remains highly challenging for current models, benchmark designers often face a fundamental trade-off. To make evaluation tractable, environments are frequently abstracted into structured states or semantic APIs (StarBench), or translated into textified interfaces like Orak. While these API-driven interaction channels offer necessary convenience, they represent a compromise that sacrifices ecological validity and alters the original capability demands.

Conversely, while APIs are a compromise for interaction, they are an unequivocal advantage for evaluation. The software accessibility of digital games enables powerful, automated assessment on the backend. Frameworks can utilize evaluator-only API bridges to compute state-verifiable progress (GameWorld) or employ memory scanning to verify quest completion (PokeGym), all without leaking privileged state information to the agent. Ultimately, recent work suggests that the benchmarking paradigm is moving toward a highly decoupled, atomic contract: preserving the rigorous, human-like observation-action interface on the front end, while exploiting the open operability of game engines for robust, automated verification on the back end (GameWorld; LMGameBench; ARCAGI3).


To provide a comprehensive overview of this landscape, this section dives into four core dimensions:
-  We introduce a five-level evolutionary framework and an orthogonal design-space view, categorizing benchmarks by game structure and evaluation intent rather than mere genre.
-  We map game environments to the specific cognitive and agentic capabilities they inherently probe, ranging from strict rule-grounding to cross-game generalization.
-  We deconstruct the interaction-evaluation pipeline, critically analyzing the trade-offs between privileged interfaces and ecological validity, alongside evaluation robustness.
-  We juxtapose the empirical bottlenecks of current VLMs/LLMs with unresolved methodological flaws, outlining directions for scalable, next-generation benchmarking.
