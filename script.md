## lead in and introduction

Static QA benchmarks and one-shot multimodal tests remain useful for measuring recognition, recall, and short-form reasoning, but they are structurally weak as evaluations of agents. They usually ask a model to produce an answer once, outside a persistent environment, with limited consequences for error and little pressure to recover from failure. Game benchmarks matter because they restore exactly the ingredients that static tests suppress: explicit rules, sequential action, delayed consequences, hidden state, opponent response, and objective progress signals. In settings such as \textit{SmartPlay}, \textit{GTBench}, and \textit{BotzoneBench}, this restoration is already enough to reveal capability gaps that static prompting tends to blur together, including legal-action failure, weak state tracking, shallow planning, and poor calibration under repeated play. The first reason to benchmark with games, then, is not that games are automatically realistic, but that they make agent behavior observable under controlled interaction.

The value of games as benchmarks becomes even clearer once the benchmark is allowed to preserve richer control loops rather than only formal rules. A benchmark such as \textit{Crafter} shows that even a single well-designed game world can package exploration, resource dependencies, long-horizon planning, and automatic scoring into one compact testbed. More recent visual and ecological benchmarks such as \textit{BALROG}, \textit{VideoGameBench}, and \textit{MCU} extend that idea toward perceptual grounding, longer trajectories, and open-ended task completion. In these settings, the benchmark no longer measures only whether a model can choose a legal move; it measures whether the model can perceive affordances, maintain useful memory, act under time pressure, and recover after earlier mistakes. This shift is a central theme of the survey: game benchmarks evolve from rule-grounded formal containers into richer probes of embodied-like agency, and each step in that evolution expands what can be measured while also complicating control and comparability.

Games are also one of the few benchmark substrates that make social and language-mediated agency behaviorally consequential. In \textit{Werewolf Arena}, success depends on persuasion, suspicion, and role-conditioned interaction under partial observability rather than on isolated text judgments. In \textit{Human-Level Diplomacy}, natural-language negotiation is not an auxiliary explanation channel but part of the strategic mechanism itself, because language changes outcomes indirectly through coalition formation and coordination. \textit{TextArena} pushes that logic further toward a living evaluation system in which repeated competitive play keeps relative differences visible through dynamic interaction rather than through a fixed static test set. These papers matter in the introduction because they show that games do not only benchmark rule following or tactical optimization; they can also operationalize deception, cooperation, negotiation, and partner modeling in ways that static benchmarks rarely make behaviorally binding.

At the broader end of the design space, recent work also sharpens a second reason to use games as benchmarks: games can evaluate adaptation, transfer, and benchmark growth in a way that fixed datasets struggle to sustain. \textit{GameVerse} is especially useful here because it treats gameplay as a closed loop of acting, failing, reflecting, and retrying, which turns games into a testbed for learning from failure rather than only for one-shot decision quality. \textit{AI GAMESTORE} makes a complementary argument at the platform level: the space of human games is broad, culturally evolved, skill-dense, and therefore much harder to saturate than a small fixed benchmark suite. Benchmarks such as \textit{Orak} and \textit{GameVerse} sit between these poles by asking whether one agentic stack can remain useful across multiple game genres rather than only within a single ruleset. Taken together, these papers suggest that the strongest modern game benchmarks are not merely harder tasks; they are attempts to turn diversity, replayability, and challenge growth into benchmark design principles.

This survey uses that development to motivate a five-level narrative taxonomy of game benchmarks for foundation model agents: from rule following, to strategic reasoning, to social intelligence, to visual agency, and finally to cross-game generalization. Our first contribution is to use these five levels as a narrative scaffold for the literature while making clear that they are not a rigid chronology; many important benchmarks straddle adjacent levels. Our second contribution is to compare benchmarks along a shared design space that includes game structure, world structure, benchmark scope, modality, and evaluation intent, so that papers can be related by more than title, venue, or game genre alone. Our third contribution is methodological: we argue that cross-paper interpretation depends not only on what capability a benchmark targets, but also on how much interface privilege it grants, how it calibrates difficulty, and how it translates trajectories into scores. Our fourth contribution is synthetic: we pair recurring model bottlenecks with recurring benchmark-design bottlenecks, then use that pairing to outline a forward agenda for more informative, comparable, and less saturable game benchmarks.

The core insight that runs through the rest of the survey is that games are valuable benchmarks because they bind cognition to action inside environments with rules, feedback, and consequences. That is why formal game benchmarks remain useful even when they are not especially human-like, and it is also why ecological and open-ended benchmarks become so attractive once the field starts asking about social agency, perception, long-horizon autonomy, and transfer. But the same progression creates the central tension in this literature: richer environments often increase ecological relevance while weakening standardization, calibration, and comparability. The remainder of the survey therefore asks four linked questions: how game benchmarks evolved across the five levels, what core capabilities they actually measure, how interaction and evaluation protocols shape the meaning of reported results, and what model failures and benchmark-design failures still block the next generation of game-based evaluation.
